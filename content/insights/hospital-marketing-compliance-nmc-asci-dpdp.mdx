Three regulators sit between an Indian hospital marketing team and the patient. The **National Medical Commission (NMC)** governs the clinical conduct of registered medical practitioners — what they can claim, how they can advertise themselves. The **Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI)** regulates the content of healthcare advertising — what creative can say, what it cannot. The **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP)** governs how patient data is collected, stored, used and erased.

Most hospital marketers know one of the three reasonably well. Almost none know all three. The result, in our experience auditing more than 60 hospital marketing operations across [Bengaluru](/bengaluru), [Mumbai](/mumbai), [Delhi NCR](/delhi-ncr), Hyderabad and Chennai, is that the average hospital marketing team is sitting on **3–7 active compliance issues** at any given moment — issues that haven't drawn complaints yet, but would not survive scrutiny.

This is the operator-level checklist. Not legal advice — go to your counsel for that — but the practical pattern of what to fix and how to document it.

## NMC — the clinical-conduct side

The NMC inherits the regulatory remit of the old Medical Council of India. Its Professional Conduct Regulations, which apply to **every registered medical practitioner**, restrict how doctors can advertise themselves and their services.

The headline rules that matter to marketing:

- **Doctors cannot solicit patients directly through advertising.** A clinical practitioner cannot run an ad that says "Dr. Sharma — Book Now." The advertising vehicle has to be the *hospital or clinic*, not the individual doctor.
- **No false or misleading claims about qualifications, services, success rates, or outcomes.** This includes implied claims — "leader in cardiac care" or "pioneers in robotic surgery" without substantiation.
- **No comparative claims against other doctors or hospitals.** No "more experienced than", no "higher success rate than".
- **Testimonials from patients are heavily restricted.** Patient endorsements that promote a specific doctor for solicitation purposes are not permitted. Patient stories that educate about a condition or treatment process — with consent — sit in a greyer zone but require careful handling.
- **No fee-based commercial endorsements** of products or services by doctors in their professional capacity.

The practical implication: marketing creative should center the **hospital brand** or the **service line**, with the doctor introduced as a clinician within that frame — not the doctor as a standalone promotional subject. This is why [doctor personal branding](/specialties) work has to be structured around education and authority, not direct solicitation.

## ASCI — the advertising-content side

The Advertising Standards Council of India is a self-regulatory body, but its **Code for Self-Regulation of Advertising** is treated as authoritative by the Department of Consumer Affairs and increasingly by the courts. Healthcare advertising is one of ASCI's most actively reviewed categories.

The provisions that recur in our audits:

- **No superlatives without substantiation.** "Best hospital", "leading", "top", "number one", "most advanced" — all require third-party evidence to support the claim, in writing, available on demand. Most hospitals can't produce it.
- **No fear-based messaging** that exploits patient anxiety. Ads showing dramatised distress, "you could die if you don't act now" framing, or shock imagery are non-compliant.
- **Comparative advertising is restricted.** You cannot say "better outcomes than X hospital" without independent, replicable data.
- **Before-and-after imagery** — especially for cosmetic, bariatric, dermatology, hair transplant and IVF — is subject to strict rules: actual patients, with documented consent, with no exaggeration of typical results, with a clearly visible disclaimer that results vary.
- **Success rate claims** require disclosure of the population, time period and definition. An [IVF](/specialties/ivf) hospital claiming "70% success rate" must specify per-cycle vs. cumulative, age band, definition (pregnancy vs. live birth), and time window.
- **Endorsements and testimonials** must be genuine, current, and the endorser must have actually used the service.

ASCI's Consumer Complaints Council reviews complaints monthly. Decisions are public. A complaint upheld against your hospital is a public-record outcome that gets picked up by health journalists.

## DPDP Act 2023 — the data-handling side

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — and the rules notified through 2025 — fundamentally reshape what hospital marketing teams can do with patient data. The high-level requirements:

- **Explicit, granular consent** for collection and use of personal data. A single "I agree to terms" tickbox is no longer sufficient for marketing use. Consent must be specific (what data, for what purpose, for how long).
- **The right to withdraw consent** and the **right to erasure**. Patients can ask you to delete their data, and you have to comply within a defined timeframe.
- **Purpose limitation**. Data collected for clinical care cannot be repurposed for marketing without separate, explicit consent.
- **A designated Data Protection Officer (or grievance officer)** with publicly visible contact details.
- **Data breach notification** to the Data Protection Board within prescribed timelines.
- **Cross-border transfer restrictions** for certain categories of data. If your CRM, email platform or analytics is hosted overseas, you need to check the data-localisation implications.

The DPDP impact on marketing is direct: every form on your website, every WhatsApp opt-in, every email subscriber, every patient who shares contact details at a health camp — all of it needs consent that holds up under the new framework. ## Where the three overlap — the dangerous middle

The trickiest compliance work is at the intersection of all three regimes. A few common scenarios:

### Patient case studies and testimonials

A patient story — say, a recovery from a complex cardiac procedure at your [cardiology](/specialties/cardiology) unit — touches all three.

- **NMC**: Does the testimonial promote a specific doctor for solicitation? If yes, problem.
- **ASCI**: Are claims about outcomes substantiated? Is the disclaimer present? Is the patient genuine?
- **DPDP**: Is there documented, specific consent from the patient for marketing use of their story, their photograph, their clinical details — separately from the clinical-care consent they signed at admission?

The compliant pattern: educational content about the condition and treatment, the patient quoted briefly with explicit consent on record, the doctor mentioned as the treating clinician without solicitation framing, a "results vary" disclaimer where outcomes are discussed.

### Doctor introduction videos

A 90-second video introducing one of your senior surgeons:

- **NMC**: The video must be presented as the *hospital* introducing its team, not the doctor advertising themselves.
- **ASCI**: No superlative claims about the doctor's standing. Substantiation for any quantified claim (years of experience, number of procedures).
- **DPDP**: If patient anecdotes appear in the video, patient consent for each.

### Success-rate claims on service-line pages

The [oncology](/specialties/oncology) team wants to put "85% 5-year survival" on the breast cancer page:

- **ASCI**: Disclosure of cohort, stage, time period, source. If it's a published study, cite it. If it's hospital internal data, the data must be available on request and presented without misleading selectivity.
- **NMC**: The claim cannot be presented in a way that solicits patients to a specific doctor.
- **DPDP**: If the data is derived from your patient records, the aggregation must be properly anonymised.

## The practical compliance checklist

Print this. Tape it to the wall behind the marketing head's desk.

### Content and claims

- [ ] All quantitative claims (success rates, experience, volume) have a sourced reference on file
- [ ] No superlatives ("best", "top", "leading", "number one") without third-party substantiation
- [ ] No comparative claims against named or implied competitors
- [ ] No fear-based or anxiety-exploitative messaging
- [ ] Before/after imagery has documented patient consent and "results may vary" disclaimer
- [ ] Patient testimonials are genuine, current, and the patient has signed marketing consent
- [ ] Doctor names appear in hospital-branded contexts, not as direct solicitation
- [ ] Doctor qualifications are accurately stated, no embellishment

### Data and consent

- [ ] Privacy policy is current and reflects DPDP requirements
- [ ] DPDP-compliant notice appears at every data collection point (forms, WhatsApp opt-ins, camp registrations)
- [ ] Marketing consent is captured separately from clinical-care consent
- [ ] Cookie consent banner is present and functional on the website
- [ ] Designated Data Protection / Grievance Officer is named with public contact details
- [ ] Process exists for handling erasure requests within prescribed timelines
- [ ] CRM and email platform vendor contracts include data-processing terms
- [ ] Cross-border data transfer is reviewed and documented for any overseas vendor

### Process and documentation

- [ ] Content brief template includes a compliance review field
- [ ] Clinical reviewer (a named doctor) signs off on all clinical content before publication
- [ ] Consent forms for patient testimonials are stored centrally and indexed by patient
- [ ] An audit log records every claim with its source, reviewer and approval date
- [ ] Crisis-response protocol exists for ASCI complaints, NMC notices, DPDP complaints

## Documentation patterns that work

The hospitals we've seen handle compliance well share a common pattern: **process beats policy**. Having a 40-page compliance policy on a server somewhere does nothing if the marketing team doesn't have working templates.

The three artifacts to build:

1. **Content brief template** with mandatory fields for clinical review status, source citations for any claim, target audience, and consent status for any patient material referenced.
2. **Clinical review log** — a simple shared sheet where every piece of marketing content is logged with the reviewing doctor's name, review date and approval status. This is your audit trail.
3. **Consent register** — a central, indexable record of all patient consents (testimonials, photographs, case studies) with consent type, date, scope and expiry. Cross-referenced to the patient ID.

These three artifacts together cover 80% of the practical compliance load.

> Compliance in hospital marketing is process, not policy. Templates beat manuals every time.

## What happens when a complaint comes in

It's worth understanding the actual processes, because they shape what you should worry about.

- **ASCI complaints** go to the Consumer Complaints Council. The CCC reviews the complaint, the advertiser's response, and any evidence. Decisions are issued within roughly 30 days and are published on ASCI's website. If a complaint is upheld, the advertiser is asked to modify or withdraw the ad. Repeated non-compliance is referred to the Department of Consumer Affairs.
- **NMC ethics complaints** go to the State Medical Council first, then potentially to NMC. Penalties for individual doctors can include warning, suspension or removal from the register. Hospitals don't get registered with NMC, but doctors named in marketing material can face individual proceedings.
- **DPDP complaints** go to the Data Protection Board through the prescribed grievance mechanism — patient first complains to the Data Protection Officer, escalates to the DPB if unresolved. Penalties for serious breaches can reach ₹250 crore.

A complaint that lands and isn't handled professionally — late response, defensive posture, denial without evidence — escalates faster than one that's handled cleanly. The first 72 hours of a complaint matter more than the substance.

## Building compliance into the workflow, not bolting it on

The hospitals that struggle with compliance treat it as a final gate — content is created, then "sent for compliance review" right before publication, where it gets stuck for a week. The hospitals that don't struggle build compliance into the brief itself. By the time a piece is drafted, the claims have been sourced, the doctor has been looped in, the consent has been checked.

This is partly a culture choice (compliance is everyone's job, not a separate department's) and partly a tooling choice (templates that force the compliance fields to be filled).
