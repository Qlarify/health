## Key Takeaways

- For most Indian hospitals, **more patients call from Google Business Profile than from the website**. Yet GBP is the most under-managed asset in healthcare marketing.
- The map pack is decided by three factors — **proximity, prominence, and relevance**. You cannot change proximity, but you can dramatically change prominence and relevance through structured, ongoing optimisation.
- The single biggest optimisation lever in 90% of hospital GBPs we audit is **category selection** — primary and secondary. Most hospitals leave this on the default and lose to competitors who set it deliberately.
- Reviews are the second biggest lever. A discharge-to-WhatsApp review request workflow run consistently for 90 days will double or triple the review volume of a mid-size multi-specialty hospital.
- **One profile per campus, not per specialty.** Hospital groups that try to create separate profiles for cardiology, oncology, and orthopaedics get suspended or de-ranked. Manage the whole campus as one verified entity with services and departments listed inside it.

## Why GBP Matters More Than Most Hospitals Realise

Pull the call analytics from any mid-size hospital in Bengaluru, Mumbai, or Delhi NCR for the last 90 days. For most hospitals, GBP calls outnumber website form fills by a factor of **3 to 8**.

Watch a patient actually search. Someone in Indiranagar types "cardiologist near me" into Google on their phone. The result is a map with three pinned hospitals, each with a "call" button. The patient taps. The website never enters the picture.

The map pack is where the decision happens for a huge proportion of urgent and semi-urgent care. GBP is the only thing that determines whether you appear in it.

> The hospital marketing director who spends ₹40 lakh on a website redesign and ₹0 on GBP is optimising the wrong asset.

## How the Map Pack Actually Works

Google's local algorithm ranks businesses on three signals. Understanding what they mean in practice tells you where to spend your optimisation effort.

### Proximity

How close the searcher is to your hospital. **You cannot influence this directly** — a patient in Whitefield sees Whitefield hospitals first. This is why each campus needs its own verified profile: proximity is calculated per location.

### Prominence

How well-known your hospital is on the wider web — review volume and recency, citations across other websites, backlinks, news coverage, NABH and JCI listings. A slow-moving signal but influenceable. Hospitals that publish steady doctor profiles, press mentions, and accreditation content build prominence over years and lock in map-pack share.

### Relevance

How well your profile matches the searcher's query. The single most controllable factor — primary and secondary categories, services listed inside the profile, content of posts, reviews, and Q&A.

A hospital that lists "Cardiology", "Cardiothoracic Surgery", and "Interventional Cardiology" as services will rank for those queries. A hospital that lists only "Hospital" as its category will not.

## Profile Fundamentals — the Foundation You Cannot Skip

Most hospital GBPs we audit fail at the basics. Before any sophisticated tactics, get these right.

### Primary category

The single most important field in the profile. It tells Google what kind of business you are.

For a multi-specialty hospital, the right primary is almost always **"Hospital"** or **"Private hospital"**. For a single-specialty centre, match the discipline — "Cardiologist", "Cancer Treatment Center", "Fertility Clinic".

Get this wrong and nothing else fixes it. We've seen 200-bed multi-specialty hospitals listed as "Medical Clinic" — a quiet ranking handicap that has cost them years of map-pack visibility.

### Secondary categories

Where most hospitals leave value on the table. Google allows up to nine. Use them.

Every major department should be represented — **"Cardiologist", "Oncologist", "Orthopaedic Surgeon", "Gynaecologist", "Paediatrician", "Emergency Care Service", "Maternity Hospital", "MRI Center"**. The principle: surface every specialty that has a patient-search query attached to it.

### Business name

Use the legal name. Do not stuff keywords ("Apollo Hospital Best Cardiologist Bengaluru"). Google will eventually penalise the profile, and patients searching for your brand will distrust the result.

### Services

Inside each category, GBP lets you list specific services. Use this aggressively. A "Cardiologist" category should have services listed: **Angioplasty, Angiography, Coronary Bypass, Heart Failure Treatment, Pacemaker Implantation, TAVR, Paediatric Cardiology**. Each entry creates a relevance match for the corresponding query.

For each service, write a 1–2 sentence description in patient language. This text is indexed and shown when the service appears in search.

### Opening hours

List actual hours per department where they differ. Most importantly, **mark the emergency department as open 24/7** — this is a strong signal for emergency-intent searches. Do not list the hospital's main number with limited hours if the ED is 24/7; you will lose night-time emergency calls to competitors.

### Attributes

Set every available attribute that genuinely applies. Wheelchair accessibility, restroom availability, online appointments, accepted insurance providers. Each attribute is a small relevance signal and an obvious patient utility.

## Photos — What to Add, What to Avoid

Google ranks profiles with active, varied photo libraries higher than profiles with stock images or sparse galleries. Add photos in these categories.

### What to add

- **Exterior**: main entrance, the campus from the road, parking entrance.
- **Interior**: reception, waiting areas, patient rooms, ICU exterior view (never patients), labs, imaging suite, OT exterior, pharmacy.
- **Doctors and staff**: clean portraits of senior clinicians with their names in the description.
- **Infrastructure**: cath lab, linear accelerator, da Vinci system, NICU equipment, dialysis unit.
- **Accreditation**: NABH plaque, JCI seal, ISO certifications.
- **Ambulance fleet**: relevant for emergency-intent searches.

### What never to add

- **Patient faces without explicit written consent**. Paediatric and oncology imagery requires extreme caution. DPDP Act and basic ethics apply.
- **Stock photos**. Google's image recognition flags these and patients see through them.
- **Overly polished marketing imagery**. The credible photos are the evidently real ones taken in your actual building.
- **Imagery that violates ASCI**. No before/after surgical photos, no exploitative imagery, no superlative captions.

Aim for **80–150 photos minimum**, refreshed monthly with 3–5 new uploads.

## Posts — the Most Underused Feature

GBP allows posts that appear in your profile and sometimes in the search results themselves. Most hospitals never use this. The ones that do see meaningful uplift in profile engagement and call-through rate.

### What to post

- **Specialist spotlights**: a short post introducing a doctor — qualifications, area of focus, OPD timings, booking link. Run one per week, rotating across departments.
- **Awareness days**: World Heart Day, Breast Cancer Awareness Month, World Diabetes Day. Tie a post to each major awareness day with a useful clinical message and a free-check-up offer where appropriate.
- **Service launches**: new equipment, new department, new accreditation. These are also legitimate news items for off-GBP coverage.
- **Health camps**: upcoming free screening camps, vaccination drives, blood donation events.
- **Patient education**: short, useful posts answering common questions. "What to bring for an MRI", "what fasting means before a blood test".

### Cadence

Aim for one post per week. Google deprecates posts after seven days from the prominent display position, so a weekly cadence keeps fresh content surfacing. Posts that include a clear photo and a call-to-action button (Book, Call, Learn More) outperform plain text posts.

## Reviews — the Single Biggest Ranking Lever

Review volume, review recency, and review response rate are among the strongest signals in the local algorithm. They are also the most under-managed asset in Indian hospital marketing.

### The discharge-to-WhatsApp review workflow

This is the highest-leverage process in GBP optimisation. Set it up once, it runs forever.

1. At discharge, capture the patient or attendant's WhatsApp number with DPDP-compliant consent.
2. **24 hours after discharge**, an automated WhatsApp message: short, sincere thank-you and a single-tap Google review link.
3. **48 hours after**, a soft reminder if no review is left.
4. Negative or moderate ratings (3 stars or below) route to patient relations for a service-recovery call before any public response.

A mid-size hospital discharging 1,500 inpatients a month, running this at a 15% completion rate, generates 225 new reviews monthly. Hospitals running no such workflow generate 5–10.

### Responding to reviews

Respond to every review. Yes, every one.

- **Positive reviews**: a personalised thank-you mentioning the specific care experience. Three to five sentences. Never copy-paste.
- **Neutral reviews**: acknowledge what could have been better, mention specific improvements being made, offer an offline contact for follow-up.
- **Negative reviews**: this is where most hospitals destroy their brand. The rules:

### What NOT to say in negative review responses

- Never disclose **any clinical detail** about the patient. This violates patient confidentiality even if the patient mentioned it themselves in their review. The DPDP Act and the NMC code both make this a serious lapse.
- Never argue or contradict the patient's account.
- Never use boilerplate ("we take all feedback seriously") — patients recognise it instantly.
- Never threaten legal action, even if the review is defamatory. Handle that privately.
- Never ask the reviewer to "remove the review" in the public response. If a resolution is reached, the reviewer will edit or remove it themselves.

### What to say

A 4-sentence template that holds up:

1. Acknowledge the patient by first name (if they used one) and thank them for raising the concern.
2. State, in general terms, that the experience described does not reflect the standard you intend.
3. Provide a named patient-relations contact and a direct phone or email.
4. Commit to investigating internally.

This response is read by every prospective patient who scrolls through your reviews. It either earns trust or destroys it. Treat it as marketing copy with the same care you would treat a paid ad.

## Q&A — Moderate Actively or Lose Control

The Questions section on GBP is open to the public. Anyone can ask a question, and anyone — including non-employees — can answer it. Hospitals that don't monitor this end up with answers from random users that are wrong, outdated, or harmful.

Two-part discipline:

- **Pre-populate** common questions yourself. "Do you accept CGHS?", "What insurance providers are empanelled?", "Is there a 24/7 emergency?", "Is there in-house pharmacy?", "Where do I park?" Answer each one as the business owner.
- **Monitor weekly**. New questions appear without notification. Set a reminder to check the Q&A section every Monday. Answer new questions within 48 hours.

Patients reading the Q&A make booking decisions based on what they see there. An unanswered or wrongly-answered question is a leaked call.

## Multi-location Group Strategy

Hospital groups with multiple campuses across Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Chennai, and Hyderabad get this wrong more often than not. Two principles:

### One profile per campus

Each physical location gets its own verified profile. Apollo Bannerghatta, Apollo Sheshadripuram, and Apollo Jayanagar are three separate profiles, each with its own reviews, photos, and posts. This is correct.

### Not one profile per specialty inside a campus

Some groups create separate profiles for "Apollo Cardiology Bannerghatta" and "Apollo Oncology Bannerghatta" at the same physical address. This violates Google's guidelines, leads to suspensions, and confuses the ranking algorithm. The right approach is one profile per campus with secondary categories for each specialty and services listed underneath.

Brand groups should also maintain consistent NAP and brand naming conventions across all campuses — "Manipal Hospitals — Whitefield", "Manipal Hospitals — Old Airport Road" — so that brand prominence accrues across the group.

## Avoiding Suspension

GBP suspensions for hospitals are common and expensive. Recovery can take weeks; sometimes the profile is never restored. Common causes:

- **Keyword stuffing in business name**.
- **Multiple profiles at the same address** (the per-specialty mistake above).
- **Frequent address or phone changes** without proper verification.
- **Fake reviews** detected by Google's spam systems, including incentivised reviews.
- **Unverified secondary categories** that don't match the services provided.
- **Inappropriate photos** — patient images without consent, ASCI-violating surgical imagery.

Run a quarterly self-audit. The cost of a suspension is far higher than a 30-minute compliance check.

## The 90-Day GBP Optimisation Plan

If your GBP has been neglected, here's the order of operations.

**Week 1**: audit the profile. Verify primary category, list all relevant secondary categories, set hours including emergency, complete attributes. Add 30+ photos across all categories.

**Weeks 2–4**: build out services under each category. Write 1–2 sentence descriptions in patient language. Pre-populate Q&A with the top 10 patient questions. Start the weekly posts cadence.

**Weeks 4–12**: set up the discharge-to-WhatsApp review workflow. Begin responding to every existing review going back six months. Add 3–5 fresh photos per week. Continue weekly posts.

**Month 3 onwards**: monitor weekly, post weekly, respond to all reviews within 48 hours, review the audit checklist quarterly. Treat the profile as you would any other strategic marketing channel — because it is one.
