A 3.8-star Google rating costs a hospital patients every single day. Not because the care is bad — often it isn't — but because online reputation management is still treated as a PR function rather than the clinical-marketing discipline it actually is.

Here is the practical reality we see across hospital clients in Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Hyderabad: the OPD front desk is world-class, the surgeons are exceptional, the NABH accreditation is framed on the wall — but the Google Business Profile has 41 reviews, the most recent is from eight months ago, and three of the visible ones are complaints that were never acknowledged. A prospective patient comparing two hospitals on their phone will choose the competitor with 340 reviews and a 4.4-star rating every time, regardless of actual clinical quality.

Reputation management done correctly is a systematic, recurring operation. This guide covers exactly how to build it.

## Where Patients Actually Check Before Booking

Before we get to tactics, we need to be honest about where Indian patients look. The priority order matters, because it tells you where to concentrate effort.

**Google Business Profile** is by far the most influential. When a patient searches "orthopaedic hospital near me" or your hospital name, your Google profile is the first thing they see. Star rating, review count, review recency — all visible without a single click. Google's own data for the South Asia region consistently shows that local search results with more than 100 reviews and a rating above 4.2 receive 70–80% of appointment clicks in competitive urban markets.

**Practo** matters disproportionately for specialist OPD bookings. Patients who are further along in their decision — they know they need a cardiologist, they are comparing three — will be on Practo. A hospital's overall rating matters less here than individual doctor ratings. We address the specialist vs. hospital brand separation later in this piece.

**JustDial** remains relevant for Tier 2 cities and for older patient demographics who still use it as a trusted local directory. Its influence on actual booking conversions has declined, but it surfaces in Google search results and a cluster of bad ratings there creates a halo problem.

**Google Maps reviews** and Business Profile reviews are the same system — we mention this because hospital administrators sometimes treat them separately and miss that negative Maps reviews are pulling down the same aggregate score.

**News and media coverage** surfaces at position 4 or 5 for most hospital name searches. A single negative news article — even one from two years ago — can persist at the top of search results and undermine every positive review signal. We include media monitoring in the stack for exactly this reason.

## The Google Review Strategy: Generating Volume Systematically

Most hospitals are reactive. They get a review, they notice it, they move on. The hospitals with 400+ Google reviews and a 4.5 rating built that asset deliberately, using a discharge-to-review workflow.

### Build the Discharge Workflow

The highest-leverage moment for a review request is 48–72 hours after discharge, when the patient is home, recovering well, and the emotional gratitude for good care is still fresh. This is not the moment to hand them a tablet at checkout — that feels institutional and the conversion rate is poor.

The workflow we recommend:

1. **Day 0 (Discharge):** WhatsApp message from the hospital (not the billing department — ideally branded as the care team). "Thank you for trusting us with your care, [Name]. We hope your recovery at home is going smoothly. Please reach out if you have any questions."

2. **Day 2:** A follow-up WhatsApp message with a single, frictionless ask. "If you're comfortable sharing your experience, a Google review helps other patients make informed decisions. It takes 60 seconds." Include the direct Google review link — not the homepage, not the Practo page, the direct GBP review link.

3. **Day 7:** If no review was left, send one more message with a different framing: "Many patients tell us they found us through a Google review from a previous patient. If we served you well, your review can help someone in the same position you were in."

That three-touch sequence, implemented via [WhatsApp Business API or an integrated CRM](/email), consistently generates review conversion rates of 8–14% of discharged patients who opted into communication. For a 100-bed hospital discharging 30 patients per day, that is 3–4 new reviews daily. In three months, the profile is transformed.

### Consent and DPDP Compliance

Under India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act, 2023)**, you need explicit consent before sending marketing or follow-up communications. The discharge consent form should include a checkbox for "post-discharge health communications via WhatsApp/SMS." Keep those consent records. Never send review requests to patients who did not consent, and never contact patients about matters unrelated to their care pathway without separate consent.

### Do Not Incentivise Reviews

ASCI guidelines and Google's own terms prohibit incentivising reviews. Do not offer discounts, free consultations, or gifts in exchange for reviews. The ask should always be presented as an act of community service — helping other patients make informed decisions.

## How to Respond to Negative Reviews Correctly

This is where most hospitals fail catastrophically. We see three common mistakes: ignoring the review entirely, responding defensively ("Our team always provides excellent care"), or — worst of all — disclosing patient details in the response to 'clarify the record.'

The correct framework is:

**Acknowledge, never argue.** The response is not for the original reviewer. It is for every future patient who reads it. A calm, professional response to a harsh review actually builds more trust than no negative reviews at all.

**Never disclose patient information.** This is not just an ethical principle — it is a legal requirement under DPDP Act and the NMC's code of ethics. Do not confirm whether the person was a patient. Do not reference their treatment, condition, bill, or any identifying detail. Full stop.

**Template structure:**
> "Thank you for sharing your experience. We take every concern seriously, and we're sorry your visit did not meet your expectations. We'd like to understand what happened so we can address it. Please contact our Patient Relations team directly at [phone/email] — we'd welcome the opportunity to speak with you."

**Follow up offline.** The goal is to resolve the issue away from the public forum. If the patient's concern is legitimate and it gets resolved, many will update or remove the review voluntarily. Never ask them to remove it as a condition of resolution — that is coercive and backfires badly when patients share the exchange publicly.

**Timing:** Respond within 48 hours. Google's algorithm factors in response activity on Business Profiles. Unanswered negative reviews also signal to prospective patients that no one is listening.

## NABH Accreditation as a Trust Signal

**NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers)** accreditation is one of the most underused trust signals in Indian hospital marketing. Most hospitals that hold it mention it once on the About page and move on.

NABH accreditation communicates something specific and credible: that your hospital meets documented standards for patient safety, clinical protocols, infection control, and staff competency. That is exactly what a patient choosing between hospitals wants to know.

**How to activate NABH as a marketing asset:**

- Display the NABH certificate logo prominently on the website homepage, on the Google Business Profile (upload as a photo), and in digital ads
- Add a dedicated page explaining what NABH means for patients: what standards were met, when the accreditation was last renewed, and what it means for their safety
- In your WhatsApp and email communications with prospective patients, reference accreditation explicitly: "Our NABH-accredited protocols mean [specific benefit — e.g., infection control rates, surgical safety checklists]"
- Train front desk and call centre staff to mention accreditation when patients ask about hospital quality

Hospitals like **Narayana Health** and **Manipal Hospitals** treat accreditation as a conversion argument, not a compliance checkbox. That framing shift matters.

## The Monitoring Stack

You cannot manage what you are not measuring. A basic reputation monitoring operation for a mid-size Indian hospital needs:

**Google Business Profile Insights** (free): Track review volume, rating trends, and profile views weekly. Set up the GBP dashboard alert so you are notified every time a new review is posted.

**Google Alerts**: Set up alerts for your hospital name, key doctor names, and common misspellings. Alerts surface news coverage, forum mentions, and blog posts that would otherwise be invisible.

**Practo Dashboard**: If you have a paid Practo listing (most mid-size and above hospitals do), the dashboard shows review activity and patient feedback. Check it weekly.

**Brand24 or Mention.com**: For hospitals with national or regional brand presence, a paid social listening tool is worth the ₹8,000–15,000 per month investment. These tools catch mentions on Reddit, Quora, YouTube comments, Twitter/X, and news sites that Google Alerts misses. Particularly useful for catching viral negative posts before they gain traction.

**Monthly reputation audit**: Once a month, someone in the marketing or patient relations team should manually review: overall star rating, review count, ratio of positive to negative in the last 30 days, whether all negative reviews received a response, and what the top patient complaints are. That last point is gold — it feeds back into clinical operations and is the data point that should go to the CMO.

## Specialist Reputation vs. Hospital Brand Reputation

This distinction matters enormously for tertiary and super-specialty hospitals. In many cases, patients are choosing **Dr. [Name]** first and the hospital second. A renowned cardiologist at a hospital with a 3.9 Google rating will still attract patients — because they are searching for that doctor, not the institution.

The implication: **individual doctor reputation management** is a separate discipline from hospital brand reputation. Doctor profiles on Practo, individual Google Knowledge Panels, and personal brand visibility on LinkedIn and YouTube require their own strategy. We cover [video as a tool for specialist authority building](/video) separately — it is one of the highest-leverage things a surgeon or specialist can do for both their personal practice and the hospital's overall brand.

The interaction between the two matters: a hospital that has invested in specialist personal brands finds that the halo effect lifts the institutional rating over time, as patients who came for a specific doctor leave reviews that reference the broader hospital experience.

## Connecting Reputation to Your Broader Marketing Stack

Reputation management does not exist in isolation. A strong Google rating amplifies the performance of every other channel:

- **Paid search ads** ([Google Ads for hospitals](/paid)) that land on a profile with 4.4 stars convert at significantly higher rates than the same ad landing on a 3.7-star profile
- **[Social media content](/social)** that surfaces real patient stories reinforces the review signals and gives prospective patients a warmer first impression before they search
- **[Email and WhatsApp campaigns](/email)** that include a review request touchpoint in the post-discharge sequence are the engine of sustainable review generation

The system compounds. More reviews → higher rating → better ad conversion → more patients → more reviews.

## FAQ

**How do hospitals manage their online reputation in India?**

Effective hospital reputation management in India involves three operations running in parallel: generating positive reviews through a systematic post-discharge communication workflow; responding to all negative reviews within 48 hours using a structured framework that acknowledges concerns without disclosing patient data; and monitoring review activity weekly across Google Business Profile, Practo, and JustDial.

**Do Google reviews affect hospital patient volume?**

Yes, materially. In competitive urban markets — Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Hyderabad — patients comparing two hospitals on Google will consistently choose the one with a higher rating and more reviews when other signals (location, specialty, insurance) are equal. The threshold effect is most pronounced at the 4.0 mark: hospitals below 4.0 stars see significantly lower click-through rates from local search results.

**How should a hospital respond to negative reviews?**

Acknowledge the concern, express regret that the experience fell short, and invite the patient to contact your Patient Relations team offline. Never argue, never disclose patient information, and never offer incentives to change or remove a review. Respond within 48 hours. The audience for your response is every future patient who reads it — not the original reviewer.

**What is the impact of NABH accreditation on patient choice in India?**

NABH accreditation directly influences patient and referring doctor decisions, particularly for elective procedures and surgical care. Patients seeking second opinions or selecting between hospitals for non-emergency procedures consistently cite accreditation as a quality signal. The marketing gap is that most NABH-accredited hospitals do not communicate what accreditation means in patient-facing language — fixing that gap is a quick trust win.
